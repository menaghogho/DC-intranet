The basics

What is GPAD?

GPAD stands for General Practice Appointments Data. It's the NHS England programme that collects appointment data directly from clinical systems in every practice and turns it into a standard, comparable national dataset.

It comes from your appointment book

Every slot booked in your clinical system — telephone, face-to-face, home visit or online — is extracted automatically. No extra forms, no separate spreadsheets.

It relies on a shared set of categories

Because every practice's local slot types are different (and often inconsistent), GPAD maps them to a small, standard set of national categories so activity can be compared fairly, practice to practice and region to region.

It's already part of your day-to-day system

When a receptionist books a slot type, or a clinician selects an outcome, they're already contributing to GPAD — the category chosen at that moment is what feeds the national picture.

It's used at every level

Individual practices use their own GPAD dashboard to understand their own workload. Primary Care Networks and Integrated Care Boards use aggregated data to plan capacity across a wider area. Nationally, it informs funding and policy decisions.

It's about accuracy, not surveillance

GPAD exists to make sure general practice's workload is properly recognised — including the parts that don't show up in a simple appointment count, like clinical admin, triage and unplanned same-day care.

Data quality is a team effort

Good GPAD data depends on everyone — reception staff selecting the right slot type, clinicians confirming or amending it, and practice managers checking the dashboard for mapping gaps.

The bigger picture

Why GPAD matters

Accurate GPAD data protects general practice by making its true workload visible — and visible workload is workload that can be planned for, funded and supported.

1

It captures the full workload

Triage calls, clinical admin and care-related contacts often go unrecorded in simple appointment counts. Correct categorisation means this work is finally counted.

2

It shapes funding and workforce decisions

Commissioners and national teams use GPAD to understand where demand is rising, and to plan staffing and investment accordingly.

3

It supports contract requirements

National categories are used to check how patients identified as clinically urgent are being managed, including same-day access commitments.

4

It gives practices their own insight

Your practice-level GPAD dashboard shows patterns in demand across the week and year — useful for your own rota and access planning, not just for reporting upward.

Field 1

Status type

How the contact actually happened. This is about the mode of contact, not what it was for — that's what context type is for, further down.

Field 2

Bookable & Allow auto DNA

Two simple switches that sit alongside the slot type, controlling how a patient can get into it and what happens if they don't show up.

Field 3

Service setting

Who is actually delivering the appointment, and under which contractual arrangement — this is what lets NHS England tell practice work apart from PCN work and outside providers.

Field 4 · the big one

Context type & national category

Every slot type maps to exactly one of these three context types, and within that, to one national category. Only Care Related Encounter categories count as an "appointment" in the official statistics — the other two exist so practices have somewhere accurate to put non-patient-facing work, not to hide it.
